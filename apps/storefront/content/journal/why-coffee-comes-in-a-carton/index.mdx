We put coffee beans in a milk carton because it looks fantastic on a counter. That is the honest answer. Everything practical about the package came after that.

Most coffee bags are designed to disappear into a cabinet. We wanted the opposite: a package you would leave out because it earns the space.

## It started with the shelf.

Coffee packaging has a familiar silhouette—soft bag, folded top, small valve, a label asking you to notice seventeen things at once. It works. It also means every bag begins to look like every other bag.

The carton gave us a simpler shape and a much bigger face. It stands up, stacks cleanly, and turns the roast name into the first thing you see.

<PullQuote>
  The carton is a flex. The fact that it stacks is a very convenient
  coincidence.
</PullQuote>

## The useful parts are still useful.

Looking good would not matter if the coffee went stale. Inside the paper shell is a food-safe barrier designed to protect the beans from oxygen and moisture. The roast date still lives where it should: easy to find, impossible to mistake for marketing copy.

<Callout title="Plain facts.">
  Same specialty-grade beans. Same roasted-to-order schedule. Considerably
  better posture.
</Callout>

## Four cartons. One system.

Light, medium, dark, and decaf use the same structure. Color does the sorting. The shape does the stacking. The front panel says what it is without making you take a tasting-notes exam.

That consistency matters more as the range grows. One carton should teach you how every carton works.

## What happens next.

We will keep improving the materials, the barrier, and the way each carton ships. We will not be replacing it with a tasteful beige pouch.

<MascotTip>Leave it on the counter. That is where it looks best.</MascotTip>
